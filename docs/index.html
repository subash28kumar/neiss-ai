Hollow points are estimates CPSC would call potentially unstable (fewer than 20 records, under 1,200, or coefficient of variation above 33%).

Is the rise just a change in coding? No. CPSC gave e-bikes their own product code in 2024, and both 2024 and 2025 use that separate code, so the 2024–2025 rise is not a coding effect. For 2019–2023, when e-bikes shared a code with mopeds, NEISS-AI uses its classifier so every year is counted the same way.

How NEISS-AI works

  1. Read every NEISS record. Each record is one emergency department visit, with product codes, the patient's age and a short injury story written by hospital staff.
  2. Label the device. Product codes and whole-word keywords in the story identify e-bikes, e-scooters, bicycles and similar devices. Before 2024 e-bikes shared code 3215 with mopeds; when the story is unclear, a text classifier trained on 2024–2025 records (where the codes are separate) decides. Cross-validated accuracy: AUC 0.995.
  3. Estimate national totals. NEISS is a stratified sample of hospitals, so each estimate uses survey weights and Taylor-linearized 95% confidence intervals, and flags unstable numbers using CPSC's rule. E-bike estimates for 2022–2024 match CPSC's published figures within 5%.
  4. Refresh automatically. When CPSC releases a new year of data, an automated job reruns the tool and updates this site.

How we know it's accurate

Three checks, all repeatable with the open code.

1. The classifier tells e-bikes and mopeds apart almost perfectly. It was tested on 2024–2025 injury stories it had not seen during training (five-fold cross-validation). Its AUC was 0.995: shown one real e-bike story and one real moped story, it ranks the e-bike story higher 99.5 times out of 100. (0.5 would be a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect.)

2. The national totals match CPSC's own published figures. The pre-2024 stories have no answer key, so the test is the total: NEISS-AI's national e-bike estimates for 2022–2024 are within 5% of the figures CPSC published in its 2025 micromobility report.

3. Car involvement and helmet use are checked on stories the rules never saw. NEISS does not code whether a motor vehicle was involved or whether the rider wore a helmet, but the injury story often says so. Since version 1.4, NEISS-AI reads both from the story. On a test set of 300 random stories (2019–2025) that were not used to build the rules, it was right for motor vehicle involvement in 98% of stories (it found 44 of 47 real cases, and 44 of its 46 flags were correct) and for helmet status in 300 of 300. Helmet status has three answers: worn, not worn, or not mentioned; "not mentioned" (about 4 in 5 stories) is never counted as "no helmet". The reference answers came from an independent AI reviewer that never saw the rules, with disagreements settled against the same written definitions; they were not made by a human reviewer.

Limits: stories are short, so a few are uncertain; NEISS covers emergency department visits only; counts are not risk rates, because the number of riders is not known.

Use the tool yourself

Free and open source (MIT licence). Download the NEISS annual files from CPSC into a folder called data, then:

pip install neissai
neissai micromobility --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data --out-dir results
neissai classify "14YOM FELL OFF HIS ELECTRIC BIKE GOING 20MPH"

Limits

Limits: NEISS counts emergency visits, not riders or miles, so these are counts, not risk rates. NEISS has no state or city information. Helmet use and motor vehicle involvement are only what the story mentions; about 4 in 5 stories do not mention a helmet. Early e-bike estimates (2019–2021) rest on small samples. The question box understands common phrases, not every possible question; check the "Understood as" line under each answer.

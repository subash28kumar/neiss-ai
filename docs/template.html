Regular e-bikes vs e-motos

Some "e-bikes" in hospital records are really electric motorcycles (e-motos, such as Sur-Ron or Talaria), or e-bikes ridden much faster than the legal limit. NEISS-AI sorts each e-bike note into three groups. Notes that clearly describe an e-moto are about 1–2% of e-bike visits each year, and that share hasn't grown. So the e-motos we can see in the notes don't explain the rise in e-bike injuries.

View

How the groups are made. Likely e-moto: the note names an electric motorcycle or dirt bike, a gas engine or an e-moto brand, or says the rider was going faster than 28 mph (the top motor-assisted speed of a legal e-bike). Unclear: the note says only "motorized bike" or "motor bike", could mean either vehicle, or says "fast" with no speed. Regular e-bike: everything else. Speeds of other vehicles, such as a car "going 30 mph", are ignored. How accurate. The rule was built on 920 notes, then locked and tested once on 240 new notes it had never seen, which had been sorted separately using written definitions. It put about 99 notes in 100 in the right group, and its share of likely e-motos (1.7%) was very close to the separately sorted answer (1.6%). When it called a note "likely e-moto", it was right about 19 times in 20. Full details, including every rule: methods note.

Read with care. "Electric bike" in a hospital note does not prove the vehicle was a legal e-bike: notes often call any two-wheeler with pedals an electric bike. A story names an e-moto only when the writer says so, so "likely e-moto" is a lower bound. A stated speed of 20 to 28 mph is not counted as an e-moto, because a legal Class 3 e-bike can give motor help up to 28 mph. CPSC blacks out brand names in the stories ("***"), so most likely e-motos are found from the rider's stated speed; an e-moto described only as an "e-bike" cannot be found. The e-bike class (1, 2 or 3) is almost never written in the story, so classes cannot be counted. Earlier years have more unclear cases because stories then often said "motorized bicycle" rather than "e-bike".

The numbers you type stay on your computer. Nothing is sent or saved. The benchmark is how many visits the state would have at the US rate for that age group (US rate x the state's population in that age group). It's an estimate from national data, not a count. Search the injury stories

Every NEISS record has a short story written by hospital emergency department staff. CPSC publishes these stories every year in its public NEISS data, a sample of about 100 US hospitals. Search the stories of the bike and scooter injuries on this site (e-bikes, e-scooters, bicycles, kick scooters, hoverboards, skateboards and mopeds; about 142,000 records from 2019 to 2025), for example battery, hit by car, helmet or delivery, to see national estimates and real examples. All words must appear.

Hazard Watch: which products started hurting more people?

NEISS-AI checks every product code in the national emergency room data (about 800 products), not just the ones people already worry about. A product is flagged when its injuries rose clearly faster than all emergency visits, after checks for chance, small numbers and coding changes. It also scans the injury stories for new words that are suddenly appearing more often.

Because about 800 products are tested at once, each flag had to pass a strict check, so few flags are false alarms (Benjamini–Hochberg method, 5%). "Check" and "Coding" flags need a person to read the stories before drawing any conclusion.

Why is the e-bike number here (102,600 in 2025) a little different from the Overview (101,800)? They are counted two ways. Hazard Watch compares CPSC product codes, so it counts records with CPSC's e-bike code. The Overview and Explore use NEISS-AI's reading of each injury story to decide which records are e-bikes. The two differ by about 1%. The same is true for e-scooters: Hazard Watch counts CPSC's powered-scooter code, while the Overview reads the stories (and, for example, leaves out scooters the story says were unpowered), so the e-scooter numbers here differ from the Overview too.

Would it have caught e-bikes early?

In a look-back test, yes, through the injury stories. We already knew e-bikes were the answer, so this shows the scan could have caught them, not that anyone would have noticed. Words such as "electric bike" and "e-bike" rose faster than all stories every year from 2021, three years before CPSC gave e-bikes their own product code in 2024. The scan also leaves over 100 other rising words each year for a person to read, so it is a list to review, not an alarm. On its own, the old moped and power-assisted cycle code (where e-bikes were counted) also rose in 2021–2023. In the all-product scan those yearly numbers were too uncertain to pass the strict test, which is why the word check matters.

Limits, and how to run it yourself

The scan is national and yearly by default. A single month is much less sensitive: for e-bikes in 2025, one month needs about a 50% rise to be detected, a full year about 28%. 2024 shows no flags because NEISS hospital weights rose sharply that year. 2020–2021 were pandemic years, so many sports and activity products swing up and down. Changes in how hospitals code injuries can look like a hazard (floors in 2025). And the scan covers emergency visits only, so every count is a floor.

pip install -U neissai
neissai download --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data
neissai hazard-export --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data
neissai hazards --years 2020-2025

How accurate is NEISS-AI?

See how NEISS-AI reads a real injury story, then see below how often it gets it right. Every check was done one of two ways: against CPSC's own published figures, or on injury stories that were set aside and never used to build the rules. Anyone can repeat them with the open code and test files.

In short: NEISS-AI's e-bike totals match the government's own published figures within 5%. When its rules were tested on injury stories they had never seen, they put about 97 to 99 stories in 100 in the right group. Two limits: the answer keys for those tests were made by AI reviewers, not people, and the e-bike/moped model hasn't yet been tested on the harder pre-2024 notes where it is used. E-scooters are different: NEISS-AI's e-scooter totals don't match CPSC's published e-scooter figures (see "E-scooter totals" further down), so use CPSC's numbers for the official e-scooter count. E-scooter totals: NEISS-AI and CPSC do not match

YearNEISS-AICPSC publishedDifference

Why they differ. From 2020 on, NEISS-AI's e-scooter count is, in effect, every NEISS record with CPSC's powered-scooter product code (5022), within about 1% each year. CPSC's published figures come from a special follow-up study. CPSC staff contacted patients and kept only cases confirmed as two-wheeled, stand-up electric scooters. They removed seated scooters, mopeds, mobility scooters, powered skateboards and scooters that weren't electric, added "unspecified scooter" cases confirmed as powered, and re-weighted the results for people who didn't answer. CPSC also revised the NEISS hospital sample in 2024 and says it adjusted the weights for earlier years. The follow-up answers aren't in the public data, so NEISS-AI can't repeat that review. That's why the gap goes both ways: NEISS-AI is below CPSC in 2019 and 2021–2022, and above it in 2023–2024. So NEISS-AI's e-scooter numbers are not reliable for trends: use CPSC's figures for e-scooter counts and trends. Read NEISS-AI's e-scooter numbers as “visits coded as powered scooters”, not confirmed e-scooters. Same CPSC source as above (Appendix A and Section III-A).

Who checked the answers

  • Done National totals were checked against CPSC's own published figures, an official source independent of NEISS-AI. E-bike totals match within 5%; e-scooter totals do not (see above).
  • Done Story-level tests (e-moto, motor vehicle, helmet) used answer keys made by AI reviewers that never saw NEISS-AI's rules and worked only from the written definitions. Where two reviewers were used, they agreed closely. But they were the same kind of system, so this is not as independent as two people reading the stories.
  • Next An outside human check. I'm looking for injury researchers to label the test stories blind, without seeing NEISS-AI's answers. I'll publish the results here, whatever they show.
  • Next Learning from people. The hardest e-bike stories are on the labelling page as Set C. When at least two approved reviewers agree on an answer, NEISS-AI uses it for that story and for near-identical ones. Safeguards: reviewers are approved by name, stories with known answers check each reviewer's care, the locked test stories are never learned from, and a new version is published only if accuracy on those locked tests doesn't drop. Every version is kept, so any change can be undone.

Known limits

  • Emergency department visits only. NEISS does not count deaths, urgent care or doctor visits, or injuries that were never treated.
  • Counts, not risk. Nobody knows how many people ride each device, so a bigger count does not mean a device is more dangerous.
  • Short stories. Each story is a sentence or two written by hospital staff, so a few cases are uncertain. "Likely e-moto" is a lower bound: an e-moto described only as an "e-bike" cannot be found.
  • E-bikes before 2024 depend on the classifier, because CPSC had no separate e-bike code until January 2024.
  • State numbers are estimates, not counts. NEISS does not report states; the Your state tab applies the national rate to each state's population.
  • Small groups are less reliable. They are flagged as unstable, using CPSC's rule.
  • Hazard Watch flags are signals to check, not proof that a product became more dangerous. NEISS weights rose about 18% in 2024, which the scan corrects for.

How NEISS-AI works

  1. Read every NEISS record. Each record is one emergency room visit, with product codes, the patient's age and a short injury story written by hospital staff.
  2. Label the device. Product codes and key words in the story identify e-bikes, e-scooters, bicycles and similar devices. Before 2024, e-bikes shared code 3215 with mopeds. When the story doesn't say which, a text model trained on 2024–2025 records (where the codes are separate) decides. On 833 unseen 2024–2025 stories it was right 98 times in 100, but on the 47 hardest of them (naming neither device, the kind it decides before 2024) only 72 times in 100. So treat any single pre-2024 record it labels as uncertain. Year totals still match CPSC's e-bike figures within 5% for 2022–2023.
  3. Estimate national totals. NEISS is a sample of about 100 hospitals, so each visit is scaled up to the whole country with CPSC's weights. Every number gets a likely range (technically, Taylor-linearized 95% confidence intervals), and numbers resting on few records are flagged using CPSC's rule. E-bike estimates for 2022–2024 match CPSC's published figures within 5%.
  4. Refresh automatically. When CPSC releases a new year of data, an automated job reruns the tool and updates this site.

Does NEISS-AI use a large language model, like ChatGPT?

No. It uses word rules plus a small, classic text model (word weights and logistic regression), trained on 2024–2025 stories where e-bikes and mopeds have separate codes. It is free to run, the same story always gets the same answer, and anyone can check the rules in the open code. The answer keys for some accuracy tests were made by AI reviewers; the Accuracy tab explains this limit.

How we know it's accurate

NEISS-AI's e-bike totals for 2022–2024 are within 5% of CPSC's own published figures, and every story-reading rule was tested once on stories it had never seen. The full report card, test files and known limits are on the Accuracy tab.

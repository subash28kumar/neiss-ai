Regular e-bikes vs e-motos

Some "e-bikes" in hospital records are really electric motorcycles (e-motos, such as Sur-Ron or Talaria) or e-bikes ridden well above legal e-bike speeds. NEISS-AI reads each e-bike story and sorts it into three groups. Likely e-motos are about 2% of e-bike visits every year, and that share has not grown, so the e-motos named in hospital stories do not explain the rise in e-bike injuries.

View

How the groups are made. Likely e-moto: the story uses e-moto wording (electric motorcycle or dirt bike, gas- or engine-powered, an e-moto brand) or gives the rider's own speed above 28 mph, the top assisted speed of a legal Class 3 e-bike. Unclear: wording that could mean either ("motorized bike", "motor bike" with no high speed, "dirt bike or e-bike"), a speed range crossing 28 mph, a downhill speed above 28 mph, or "fast" with no speed given. Diagnosis coding phrases are ignored. Regular e-bike: everything else. Speeds of other vehicles ("struck by a car going 30 mph") are ignored. Accuracy. The rule was built on 320 stories, then locked and tested once on 240 new stories from 2019–2025 that it had never seen. The test stories were labelled blind against written definitions, without seeing the rule. Across all e-bike stories the rule puts 98% in the right group (95% range 97–99%), and it estimates the share of likely e-motos almost exactly (2.0% by the rule vs 2.0% by the blind labels). Single "likely e-moto" calls are right about 3 times in 4: most misses are "motorized bicycles" ridden over 28 mph, which the definitions count as unclear. Full details: methods note.

Read with care. A story names an e-moto only when the writer says so, so "likely e-moto" is a lower bound. CPSC blacks out brand names in the stories ("***"), so most likely e-motos are found from the rider's stated speed; an e-moto described only as an "e-bike" cannot be found. The e-bike class (1, 2 or 3) is almost never written in the story, so classes cannot be counted. Earlier years have more unclear cases because stories then often said "motorized bicycle" rather than "e-bike".

Hazard Watch: which products started hurting more people?

NEISS-AI checks every product code in the national emergency room data (about 800 products), not only products someone already suspects. A product is flagged when its injuries rose clearly faster than all emergency visits, after checks for chance, small numbers and coding changes. It also reads the injury stories for new words that start appearing more often.

Why is the e-bike number here (102,600 in 2025) a little different from the Overview (101,600)? They are counted two ways. Hazard Watch compares CPSC product codes, so it counts records with CPSC's e-bike code. The Overview and Explore use NEISS-AI's reading of each injury story to decide which records are e-bikes. The two differ by about 1%.

Would it have caught e-bikes early?

Yes, through the injury stories. Words such as "electric bike" and "e-bike" rose faster than all stories every year from 2021, three years before CPSC created a separate e-bike product code in 2024. Checked on its own, the old moped and power-assisted cycle code (where e-bikes were counted) also rose in 2021–2023; in the all-product scan those yearly numbers were too uncertain to pass the strict test, which is why the word check matters.

Limits, and how to run it yourself

National and yearly by default: a single month is much less sensitive (for e-bikes in 2025, one month needs about a 50% rise to be detected, a full year about 28%). 2024 shows no flags because NEISS hospital weights rose sharply that year. 2020–2021 are pandemic years, so many sports and activity products swing up and down. Coding practice can look like a hazard (floors in 2025). Emergency visits only, so all counts are a floor.

pip install -U neissai
neissai download --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data
neissai hazard-export --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data
neissai hazards --years 2020-2025

How NEISS-AI works

  1. Read every NEISS record. Each record is one emergency department visit, with product codes, the patient's age and a short injury story written by hospital staff.
  2. Label the device. Product codes and whole-word keywords in the story identify e-bikes, e-scooters, bicycles and similar devices. Before 2024 e-bikes shared code 3215 with mopeds; when the story is unclear, a text classifier trained on 2024–2025 records (where the codes are separate) decides. Cross-validated accuracy: AUC 0.995.
  3. Estimate national totals. NEISS is a stratified sample of hospitals, so each estimate uses survey weights and Taylor-linearized 95% confidence intervals, and flags unstable numbers using CPSC's rule. E-bike estimates for 2022–2024 match CPSC's published figures within 5%.
  4. Refresh automatically. When CPSC releases a new year of data, an automated job reruns the tool and updates this site.

How we know it's accurate

Three checks, all repeatable with the open code.

1. The classifier tells e-bikes and mopeds apart almost perfectly. It was tested on 2024–2025 injury stories it had not seen during training (five-fold cross-validation). Its AUC was 0.995: shown one real e-bike story and one real moped story, it ranks the e-bike story higher 99.5 times out of 100. (0.5 would be a coin flip; 1.0 is perfect.)

2. The national totals match CPSC's own published figures. The pre-2024 stories have no answer key, so the test is the total: NEISS-AI's national e-bike estimates for 2022–2024 are within 5% of the figures CPSC published in its 2025 micromobility report.

3. Car involvement and helmet use are checked on stories the rules never saw. NEISS does not code whether a motor vehicle was involved or whether the rider wore a helmet, but the injury story often says so. Since version 1.4, NEISS-AI reads both from the story. On a test set of 300 random stories (2019–2025) that were not used to build the rules, it was right for motor vehicle involvement in 98% of stories (it found 44 of 47 real cases, and 44 of its 46 flags were correct) and for helmet status in 300 of 300. Helmet status has three answers: worn, not worn, or not mentioned; "not mentioned" (about 4 in 5 stories) is never counted as "no helmet". The reference answers came from an independent AI reviewer that never saw the rules, with disagreements settled against the same written definitions; they were not made by a human reviewer.

Limits: stories are short, so a few are uncertain; NEISS covers emergency department visits only; counts are not risk rates, because the number of riders is not known.

Use the tool yourself

Free and open source (MIT licence). Download the NEISS annual files from CPSC into a folder called data, then:

pip install neissai
neissai micromobility --years 2019-2025 --data-dir data --out-dir results
neissai classify "14YOM FELL OFF HIS ELECTRIC BIKE GOING 20MPH"

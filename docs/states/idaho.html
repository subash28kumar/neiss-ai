E-bike injuries in Idaho

If people in Idaho were hurt at the national rate for their age group, about 610 would have gone to an emergency room after an e-bike injury in 2025 (range 440 to 780, from the national estimate). That is about 2 a day, or 1 in 3,300 residents, and about 15 times the 2020 level.

These are expected numbers, not counts. NEISS does not record the patient's state, so no one can count Idaho's visits from it. NEISS-AI takes the national rate for each age group and applies it to Idaho's population. The real number can be higher or lower, for example where more or fewer people ride. The range shows only the uncertainty in the national number, not how Idaho differs from other states.

How it is worked out: for each age group, the national e-bike ER visit rate × Idaho's population in that age group, then added up. See it step by step.

What this number is for
  • Good for: a rough local figure for grant applications, reports and safety plans, and a benchmark to check your own hospital counts against.
  • Not for: ranking states or judging how safe Idaho is. Every state gets the same national rate for each age group, so differences between states come only from population size and age mix, not from local riding or road safety.

Public health and transportation

  • Hospital stays: about 97 of the 2025 e-bike visits likely ended in a hospital stay (national hospital-stay shares by age applied to Idaho).
  • Who: the most visits were among people aged 25-44.
  • Road deaths (real count): in 2024, 3 bicyclists, including e-bike riders, were killed in traffic crashes with motor vehicles in Idaho: 1.3% of all traffic deaths, 0.15 per 100,000 people, lower than the US rate of 0.32. Source: NHTSA FARS.
  • Biking to work (real survey): about 8,500 people in Idaho usually bike to work (0.88% of workers; US 0.47%), Census ACS 2025. More riding usually means more bike injuries.
  • Medical bills (estimate): e-bike injuries in Idaho in 2025 likely cost about $4.9 million to $11 million in medical care (2025 dollars). The low number is if every injury cost like a fall with no car; the high number is if every injury cost like a crash with a car. Source: CDC study.

How the number is worked out

In one line: for each age group, the national e-bike ER visit rate × Idaho's population in that age group, then added up. The example below uses ages 25–44.

  1. Count the national visits for each age group. NEISS samples about 100 hospitals. Each sampled visit stands for many visits across the country, using CPSC's weights (for e-bikes in 2025, about 43 visits each on average). Adding them up gives the national number. Example: about 30,700 e-bike ER visits by people aged 25–44 in 2025.
  2. Turn it into a rate. Divide by the number of people that age in the whole US (US Census Bureau). Example: 30,700 ÷ 91.9 million people aged 25–44 = about 33.4 visits per 100,000 people.
  3. Apply the rate to Idaho. Multiply the rate by Idaho's population in the same age group. Example: 33.4 per 100,000 × 523,699 people aged 25–44 in Idaho ≈ 175 visits.
  4. Add up all seven age groups (under 5, 5–13, 14–17, 18–24, 25–44, 45–64 and 65+). For Idaho in 2025, that gives about 610.

Formula: Idaho visits = the sum, over age groups, of (national visits in that age group ÷ US population in that age group) × Idaho's population in that age group.

The range (440 to 780) comes only from the uncertainty in the national estimate. The method assumes people in every state are hurt at the same rate for their age, so it cannot show differences in how much people ride, local roads or rules.
